# Ex ante modelling of productivity, competition and GDP effects of reforms: precedents for an interactive policy-costing tool

Research note, 22 Oct 2026. Every figure below comes from a source I reached; URLs are listed at the end. Anything I could not check against a primary source is marked UNVERIFIED.

## 0. Corrections to the brief and verification flags

- **PC "National Competition Policy: modelling proposed reforms" is a 2024 report, not 2025.** It was sent to government on 1 Nov 2024 and released on 29 Nov 2024. The PC re-hosted the files in Oct 2025.
- **PC 2005 headline (2.5% of GDP, about $20bn) is verified.** It is *ex post* modelling of productivity and price changes observed in seven infrastructure sectors between 1989-90 and 1999-00. It is not a projection, and the PC says it includes the effects of non-NCP factors.
- **"Competition in Australia: too little of a good thing?" is a Grattan Institute report** (Minifie, Dec 2017), not a Treasury paper. I saw only a listing for it; its content is UNVERIFIED. Treasury's markup work is Hambur (2021) and Hambur & Freestone (2025).
- **Harper Review Recommendation 56** asked the PC to model the package. PC (2024) says this was not implemented, so there is no Harper-era modelling.
- **Five pillars final reports were released on 19 Dec 2025.** The interim reports came out between 31 Jul and 13 Aug 2025.

## 2. How PC (2024) turned policies into shocks

This is the most reusable template for the calculator. Every shock was a chain of explicit factors (PC Tables C.5–C.9).

- **Distribution networks (second-hand car imports):** import price of Motor Vehicles & Parts −0.098%, built as 11% × 20% × 10% × 45%. Tariff/compliance stream: midpoint saving $2.58bn of a $1.29–3.87bn range = 0.06% of industry costs, applied as +0.06% MFP in all industries plus tariff removal.
- **Restraint of trade (L1):** labour productivity +0.24% in 24 service industries and +0.14% elsewhere (agriculture excluded). These are 10% of the Johnson-Lavetti-Lipsitz (2023) US wage effects (2.4% and 1.4%). GDP +$5.1bn; the 50% case gives $2.6bn. With e61's 4% wage gap as the input, the GDP gain was 105% larger.
- **Occupational licensing (L2):** +0.8% labour productivity in 15 industries. This comes from an OECD elasticity (a 1-unit stringency cut gives +1.6% labour productivity) with a 0.5-unit cut assumed. Results are linear: 100/75/50/25% shocks give GDP +0.39/0.29/0.19/0.10%.
- **Banking (D4):** a 0.5pp mortgage-rate cut is equivalent to a 3.99% lower repayment, i.e. a $3.267bn rent removed from finance used by the ownership of dwellings industry. SME loan spreads give a further $1.878bn rent. Payments (D5): $1.372bn rent.
- **Public procurement (B3):** 2% of $225bn procurement treated as rents, with real government spending held fixed.
- **Telehealth (H5):** 14.1m hours saved × 0.643 employed × 0.75 not taken as leisure = 6.8m hours, over 23.5bn total hours → labour productivity +0.03%.
- **Elasticity cases:** retail capital productivity +0.1% → GDP +$23m (0.0009%).
- **Closure:** long-run (≈10 yrs) closure with fixed aggregate employment, fixed rate of return (foreign capital adjusts), fixed tax rates and fixed savings rate; the nominal exchange rate is the numeraire so CPI can move.
- **Published results workbook:** "PC National CGE model" XLSX, with one tab per simulation plus `_lower` and linearity-test tabs, covering 114-industry, GSP and revenue results. It is a results workbook, not a runnable model.

## 3. Lessons for an interactive calculator

1. **Use two layers.** Layer A maps policy features to a *direct-effect shock* (productivity, price, rent or output by industry). Layer B maps the shock to economy-wide effects (GDP, CPI, wages, revenue, GSP). The PC kept these separate and published both. The calculator should show Layer A arithmetic as editable chains of factors (share × share × effect), each with its own source.
2. **Use pre-computed elasticities rather than live CGE runs.** PC (2024) found results "roughly… linearly related to the shock". Its tests at 25–100% confirm this. GDP and CPI responses per 1% shock by industry can therefore be precomputed from the PC workbook or VURM runs and scaled. Simpler fallbacks: PC (2017) says GDP ≈ 1.1 × the productivity shock weighted by sector size. My own back-calculation from NSW (2021) gives about 1.6× ($1.2bn GSP from a $750m cost cut, by 2041).
3. **Tag each reform by evidence type** (direct, case study, scenario/"outer envelope", illustrative elasticity) and grade the evidence, as CBAx does (low/medium/high plus success probability). Show "outer envelope" results with a visible warning.
4. **Default to ranges, and apply a realisation haircut.** The PC reported lower and upper bounds (often a 50% shock), and two models (CGETAX 0.7% vs VURMTAX 0.4% of GDP). On ex ante versus ex post, IC 1995's 5.5% became PC 2005's 2.5%: "less than half". Add a user-adjustable implementation or realisation factor. The UK calculator's low/high combination of extremes is a simple pattern.
5. **Separate efficiency from transfers.** Rent removal (banking, procurement, repairs) lowers prices but only the reallocation effect raises GDP (PC 2017; PC 2024 rent modelling). Report price/CPI, GDP and income (GNI, wages) separately, because they diverge. In PC 2005 household purchasing power rose 1.2% against GDP's 2.5% because of terms-of-trade and investment effects.
6. **Keep compliance-cost savings separate from productivity or GDP effects.** Standard Cost Model (SCM) arithmetic (P × Q; Dutch €56/hr, Victorian $71.76/hr examples; German case-count method) gives partial-equilibrium burden savings. A separate, labelled step converts them to GDP. The PC's $10bn target has no GDP translation, so any GDP figure for it would be the tool's own assumption and must be flagged.
7. **Model time paths, not only the long run.** Égert & Gal give 5-year, 10-year and long-run effects (ETCR reform: 0.72%, 1.02%, 2.09%). CGE long-run closures assume about 10 years. CBAx uses cohorts, lags, duration and ramp-up. Show when gains arrive, and where relevant discount them at selectable rates (NZ 5% and 2%; UK now 3%).
8. **Keep the static budget costing separate from dynamic revenue.** The PBO excludes economy-wide effects without compelling evidence. The PC reports revenue gains that come from GDP growth, excluding implementation costs. Show "static fiscal cost", "behavioural" and "second-round revenue" as separate lines, and split Commonwealth from state revenue. This matters for National Productivity Fund-style payments: ≈$100m+ allocated by 1 May 2026.
9. **Distribution.** Give results by industry (114 in PC National and TIM), by state (GSP, as in PC 2024 Table 4) and by household type (PC 2005 used an income-distribution model). State results depend mainly on industry structure, not differing shocks.
10. **Indicator-based mode.** Let users move an OECD-style indicator (PMR/ETCR, OER stringency) and apply published elasticities (OECD 1.6% per unit; EC PMR-to-markup elasticities). Warn that these are cross-country averages (Égert & Gal) and that adding up many reforms simultaneously is "rather implausible".
11. **Stop headline creep.** The government's non-compete figure ("$5bn or 0.2% of GDP annually") is the PC's upper bound of a scenario with an arbitrary 10% pass-through. Exported outputs should carry the bound, method tag and key assumption with them.
12. **Make transparency the default:** a sources database of values (CBAx has ≈200 monetised impacts), shock-derivation tables, a "non-quantified benefits" column (PC Table 3 "Other"), sanity flags (CBAx: ROI >5 suggests overestimation), and a "reverse analysis" mode ("what shock is needed to break even?").
